Why I’m doing this

Every minute, somewhere, an alert fires in a Security Operations Center. Someone has to decide in minutes whether it’s noise or the start of a breach. I want to be that person.

Today I’m starting a 6-month challenge: go from studying to interview-ready for a SOC Analyst role, and document every step here.

What employers actually want

I collected seven real SOC job descriptions from the region and mapped every requirement. The same skills showed up again and again:

  • Triaging alerts in a SIEM (Splunk, Microsoft Sentinel, QRadar, Rapid7)
  • Writing search queries in SPL and KQL
  • Reading Windows event logs, Sysmon and process trees
  • Understanding Active Directory and Kerberos attacks
  • Networking: TCP/IP, DNS, proxy and firewall logs
  • EDR/XDR: investigating endpoints and isolating hosts
  • Mapping findings to MITRE ATT&CK
  • Phishing analysis and IOC enrichment

Most of them also ask for 3+ years of experience. I can’t fast-forward time, but I can build real, provable hands-on work.

My plan: 26 weeks, two hours a day

Month Focus
1 SOC fundamentals, networking, Windows/Linux internals, building my lab
2 SIEM: Splunk SPL, Microsoft Sentinel KQL, Windows log hunting
3 MITRE ATT&CK, Active Directory attacks, phishing, malware and process trees
4 EDR, incident response end to end, detection engineering, threat hunting
5 Python/PowerShell for SOC, cloud identity, first certification (SC-200)
6 CV, blue-team challenges, mock interviews, applications

Learning by doing: my home SOC lab

Reading about attacks isn’t enough. I’m building a small lab: a Windows domain controller, a Windows workstation with Sysmon, Wazuh and Splunk as my SIEMs, and a Kali machine to attack my own network. Then I’ll try to catch myself.

Every attack I run becomes a detection I write and a post I share.

How I’m using AI as a mentor

I’m using Claude as a daily study partner. Each two-hour evening session follows the same rhythm:

  1. 10 min — quiz on previous days
  2. 20 min — learn today’s topic through a real-world incident example
  3. 30 min — hands-on in my home lab
  4. 30 min — a log drill with a hidden attack, or a TryHackMe room
  5. 20 min — interview questions on the day’s topic, graded
  6. 10 min — save everything to my Obsidian vault, plus flashcards

The best part: AI can generate realistic alerts and log files with a hidden attack for me to find, then grade my answer like a senior analyst would.
